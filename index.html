How to use this guide

Every event, claim and quotation in this guide is tied to the novel. References take the form 7 (40%): Chapter 7, about 40 per cent of the way through the chapter. Positions are rounded to the nearest 5 per cent. This works in any edition: find the chapter, then estimate how far into it you need to go. Ranges look like 12 (35–100%).

Two ways to read the banks. The event bank and quote bank run in chapter order, so you can revise one chapter at a time or read straight through for the arc of the whole novel. The theme arcs group the seventeen chapters into four movements: arrival and first torments (1–4), Hang Wood and its aftermath (5–10), the shed, the castle and Fielding (11–14), and the closing of the trap (15–17).

Context

Facts about the writer and period are attributed to a named source. Facts about the work come from the work.

Where the novel began

Hill writes that in the summer of 1969 she rented a cottage on a farm in a remote part of Dorset, beside an ancient wood actually called Hang Wood, with deer coming down to a stream.

Source: Susan Hill, Afterword

The two boys

She describes watching the farmer’s grandson and his friend, two prep-school boys who seemed the best of friends, and says her two characters grew out of them, although they were nothing like Kingshaw and Hooper. By the end of the holiday the book was begun and outlined.

Source: Susan Hill, Afterword

A book about children, written for adults

Hill says the novel is mainly about children but was written as an adult for adults, and names Dickens, Katherine Mansfield and Elizabeth Bowen as writers who did the same. She also reports that it has long been a GCE and GCSE set book and that young readers often find it truer than their parents do.

Source: Susan Hill, Afterword

Hill on the ending

Asked whether a child would really take his own life, Hill answers that children have and do, and that she still sees the ending as inevitable for Kingshaw.

Source: Susan Hill, Afterword

Hill on what the novel is about

She calls it a novel about cruelty and the power of evil, but most of all about isolation and the lack of love: no one in it is loved, and the adults are made selfish and blind by that lack. She singles out Fielding as the one boy who has known ordinary love, and says he has no power to save the others.

Source: Susan Hill, Afterword

Susan Hill

Hill was born in Scarborough and published her first novel while still a student, the year she began at King’s College London. From the late 1960s she wrote a run of acclaimed novels, of which this, published in 1970, was one; her most popular books are ghost stories and crime fiction, and The Woman in Black (1983) became a stage play that has run in the West End for more than twenty years. LitCharts dates the writing to 1969, in line with Hill’s own account of the summer of 1969, though it names Stratford-upon-Avon and London rather than Dorset as places of writing.

Source: LitCharts, Brief Biography and Key Facts p. 1; Susan Hill, Afterword

The Gothic tradition

LitCharts places the novel in the tradition of Gothic fiction: an atmosphere of fear and gloom, an isolated old house that seems to have a will of its own, and a forbidden room. It notes that Jane Eyre also has a red-room, so Hill’s Red Room reads as a deliberate echo. Warings itself is old, gloomy and cut off 1 (30%).

Source: LitCharts, Introduction and Symbols (Warings, the Red Room)

Children capable of evil

LitCharts compares the novel with William Golding’s Lord of the Flies (1954): both are told through children but are not children’s books, and both show children as psychologically complex and capable of cruelty. Hill makes a similar point in the Afterword when she says the book was written for adults.

Source: LitCharts, Introduction; Susan Hill, Afterword

Property and class

LitCharts reads the boys’ conflict through English property and class. The text supports the starting point: the Hooper estate has been sold off, piece by piece, leaving only the house 1 (30%), and Hooper values it for what owning it means rather than for itself 1 (35%). The wider history LitCharts gives (enclosure and the rise of landed estates) is background, not something the novel mentions.

Source: LitCharts, Historical Context and Themes

Using the Afterword in an answer

The Afterword is the author’s later reflection, not part of the novel. Quote it sparingly and treat it as one view: Cambridge rewards your reading of the text (AO4), and Hill herself says she does not fully understand the book.

Source: Susan Hill, Afterword; 0475 syllabus, assessment objectives (p. 10)

Event bank

38 numbered events in order. Read one chapter for close revision, or E1 to E38 straight through as the arc of the whole work.

Chapter 1

E1 The dying grandfather 1 (0–5%)

Edmund Hooper is taken to see his grandfather, who is dying upstairs after a second stroke 1 (0%). Joseph Hooper had hoped the meeting would be moving; it is not 1 (5%). Edmund’s only comment compares the old man to one of his dead moths 1 (10%).

Why it matters. The novel opens on death treated without feeling, and introduces the moths before the Red Room is shown.

E2 Warings, the Red Room and the father 1 (10–90%)

Joseph Hooper, now master of the house, plans to find his son a companion 1 (15%). He keeps the key of the Red Room, where his father’s cases of moths and butterflies are displayed 1 (70%). Edmund takes the key at night and goes in alone 1 (65%).

Why it matters. The house and its collection belong to the Hoopers; the moths become Hooper’s first weapon.

Chapter 2

E3 The note 2 (0–25%)

Mrs Helena Kingshaw and Charles arrive. Hooper wraps a message round a lump of plasticine and drops it from his window 2 (20%). Kingshaw reads it in the hall: Hooper did not want him to come 2 (25%).

Why it matters. Hostility is declared before the boys have met. Hill gives it no cause.

E4 First questions, first fight, the tour 2 (25–100%)

Hooper interrogates Kingshaw about his flat and his dead father 2 (30%). When Kingshaw claims the bedroom window as his own, they fight 2 (45%) 2 (45%). Ordered to show him round, Hooper runs through the house listing rooms, and on the back stairs Kingshaw has the thought of pulling him over, and does not act on it 2 (100%).

Why it matters. Ownership is the ground of the conflict from the start: whose house, whose room.

Chapter 3

E5 The crow in the cornfield 3 (0–25%)

Walking alone towards Hang Wood, Kingshaw is attacked by a crow in the cornfield 3 (25%). Lying winded, he sees a figure at a top window 3 (20%); when the crow has gone, the narrator names the watcher: Hooper, at his bedroom window 3 (25%).

Why it matters. Chance supplies the fear, and Hooper has watched all of it.

E6 The stuffed crow on the bed 3 (50–60%)

That night Kingshaw finds a stuffed crow on his bed 3 (55%). He does not scream 3 (55%).

Why it matters. The second crow shows that the first was watched. Kingshaw’s silence becomes his habit.

E7 Locked in the Red Room 3 (65–90%)

Hooper takes Kingshaw into the Red Room among the moth cases 3 (70%) and locks him in. Kingshaw will not shout 3 (85%) and is sick. Next morning Hooper denies knowing he was there 3 (90%).

Why it matters. The method is set: Hooper uses what Kingshaw fears and leaves no evidence.

Chapter 4

E8 The room with the dolls 4 (0–10%)

Kingshaw finds an unused room with a cabinet of old dolls, works on his galleon model there and locks the door 4 (5%).

Why it matters. His first defence copies Hooper’s: withdrawal behind a locked door.

E9 The train to London; the adults misread the boys 4 (20–100%)

On the train, Hooper reads a horror comic while his father reflects on him 4 (20%). Hooper finds the hidden room and the things Kingshaw has been gathering, guesses his plan to run away, and announces that he will go too 4 (85%). The adults congratulate themselves on how well the boys get on 4 (100%).

Why it matters. Hill sets what the boys know against what the adults believe, in the same chapter.

Chapter 5

E10 Running away 5 (0–70%)

With the adults away in London for the day 5 (0%), Kingshaw sets his alarm for five and leaves at dawn with his savings and supplies 5 (10%). He is frightened, not adventurous 5 (30%).

Why it matters. Escape is planned with care and carried out in fear: Hill refuses the boys’ adventure story.

E11 Into Hang Wood 5 (70–95%)

Kingshaw reaches the edge of the wood 5 (70%) and goes in.

Why it matters. The wood is unowned ground, outside Warings.

Chapter 6

E12 Hooper has followed 6 (0–95%)

Inside the wood Kingshaw discovers that Hooper has followed him 6 (25%). They track a deer, and when the way is lost Kingshaw hears fear in Hooper’s voice for the first time 6 (95%).

Why it matters. The escape fails because it was watched, but the wood gives Kingshaw a competence he has nowhere else.

Chapter 7

E13 The storm 7 (0–15%)

A storm breaks; Hooper is terrified 7 (10%) while Kingshaw stays calm 7 (10%). Afterwards Kingshaw believes the storm has changed things between them 7 (15%), and the narrator adds that he has forgotten what Hooper is like.

Why it matters. The first reversal of power, and Hill marks its limits at once.

E14 The dead rabbit; lost 7 (20–65%)

Hooper taunts Kingshaw over a dead rabbit 7 (20%). When Hooper panics at being lost he beats the ground and screams 7 (65%), and Kingshaw slaps him across the face to stop him 7 (65%).

Why it matters. Each boy is shown in his own fear; neither is simply strong.

E15 The stream: Hooper hurt 7 (80–95%)

Exploring alone with a ball of string, Kingshaw thinks of walking away and leaving Hooper 7 (80%), but feels responsible for him 7 (85%) and goes back. He finds Hooper face down in the stream, bleeding from the head 7 (85%), hauls him out and lights the fire 7 (95%).

Why it matters. Kingshaw behaves well when he could leave; the novel will not reward it.

Chapter 8

E16 Night in the wood 8 (0–100%)

Kingshaw calls Hooper a bully for his cruelty to a fish 8 (15%), and then finds himself taunting Hooper in turn 8 (85%). Hooper has a nightmare and cries for his mother 8 (55%), and makes Kingshaw promise not to leave him. Kingshaw feels something has changed 8 (100%).

Why it matters. The fullest view of Hooper as a frightened child, and a hold that will not last the morning.

Chapter 9

E17 Found 9 (0–85%)

The searchers come the next morning 9 (0%). Kingshaw, lying in the stream, does not want to be found 9 (85%).

Why it matters. The water and the wood are where he is at peace, which prepares the ending.

Chapter 10

E18 Hooper’s lie 10 (0–25%)

Hooper tells the adults that Kingshaw pushed him into the water 10 (0%). The adults believe Hooper, and Kingshaw’s furious reply is heard as spite 10 (25%).

Why it matters. The decency of the wood is erased in a sentence. Truth carries no weight with the adults.

E19 The little talk 10 (65–100%)

Kingshaw realises his mother will marry Mr Hooper 10 (65%). When he says he hates Hooper, she calls it wicked talk 10 (90%). She tries to have a talk with him 10 (70%) and offers to sort out any small problem 10 (85%). Alone, he decides that only the wood is left to him 10 (100%).

Why it matters. The gap between what adults offer and what the boy needs is complete; the ending is now in view.

Chapter 11

E20 The new school 11 (0–35%)

At breakfast Kingshaw learns he will go to Hooper’s school 11 (15%).

Why it matters. The last exit, a return to his old school, is closed.

E21 Locked in the shed 11 (10–95%)

Kingshaw hides in a shed in the fields and is locked in 11 (15%). Through the walls Hooper’s whisper tells him what will happen at the new school 11 (75%). At lunchtime Hooper unlocks the door and runs off 11 (95%).

Why it matters. Hooper’s threats move from the house to the future: the school becomes a second Warings.

Chapter 12

E22 Leydell Castle 12 (0–35%)

On a family outing to a ruined castle 12 (0%), Kingshaw climbs the walls and shouts the title rhyme 12 (35%).

Why it matters. The title becomes literal, and Kingshaw, not Hooper, says it.

E23 The fall 12 (65–100%)

Hooper has climbed after him and freezes with fear 12 (75%). Kingshaw talks him down, knows he could make him fall and knows he will not 12 (95%). When Kingshaw reaches out, Hooper flinches, steps back and falls 12 (100%).

Why it matters. Kingshaw’s one act of mercy looks, from below, like the act he refused to commit.

Chapter 13

E24 Ignored 13 (0–10%)

Afterwards Kingshaw remembers being ignored 13 (0%). He knows that from a distance the fall would have looked like a push 13 (10%).

Why it matters. Kingshaw is invisible to the adults even at the centre of events.

E25 King of the Castle at night; the drive home 13 (50–75%)

Left with Mrs Boland, Kingshaw imagines Warings without Hooper 13 (70%). Driving home at midnight, Mr Hooper tells Mrs Kingshaw there is to be no blame 13 (75%).

Why it matters. Hill cuts from the boy’s hope to the adults’ comfort without comment.

E26 The nightmare 13 (80–100%)

Kingshaw wakes from a nightmare believing Hooper dead 13 (95%). The adults soothe him 13 (95%); Mr Hooper carries him back to bed, and he is comforted and ashamed 13 (100%).

Why it matters. The only physical comfort he receives comes from Hooper’s father.

Chapter 14

E27 Mrs Kingshaw at the hospital 14 (0–35%)

His mother visits Hooper daily and asks Kingshaw for a message and a present 14 (20%).

Why it matters. The mother’s attention moves to the other boy.

E28 The church 14 (35–45%)

Alone while Hooper is in hospital, Kingshaw goes into the empty village church 14 (35%) and prays that nothing will happen 14 (40%), admitting he fears Hooper more than anything in the world 14 (40%).

Why it matters. Even prayer turns back to Hooper. The scene ends with the arrival of Fielding.

E29 Fielding 14 (45–90%)

Kingshaw meets Fielding, a farmer’s son, who shows him a calf being born 14 (60%). Fielding is easy and unafraid 14 (85%). On the farm Kingshaw watches birds being killed and holds Fielding’s hamster 14 (70%) 14 (75%). Told about Hooper, Fielding cannot see the danger 14 (80%).

Why it matters. Ordinary goodness appears, and has no power to help.

Chapter 15

E30 Hooper returns 15 (0–65%)

Hooper comes home and knows everything 15 (0%). Mrs Kingshaw has become sharper with Charles 15 (35%). Hooper has learned about Fielding through her 15 (65%).

Why it matters. Even the new friendship is passed to Hooper by the adults.

E31 The uniform 15 (40–85%)

Kingshaw goes to London with Mr Hooper to buy the uniform of the new school 15 (40%). In the mirror, dressed like Hooper, he sees there is no hope 15 (85%).

Why it matters. The uniform makes the future visible and identical to Hooper.

E32 The model, the blow and his mother’s verdict 15 (85–100%)

Kingshaw finds Hooper playing with the helter-skelter fort he made, which the adults have given away 15 (90%). When he protests, Mr Hooper strikes him across the cheek 15 (90%). Hooper smashes the model, and his mother, arriving at the door, tells Kingshaw he should be ashamed 15 (100%).

Why it matters. The adults’ blindness becomes open injustice: the one adult act of violence in the novel falls on the wrong boy.

Chapter 16

E33 The wedding news; the circus 16 (0–55%)

On the telephone Mrs Kingshaw hints at her plans 16 (5%). Hooper tells Kingshaw that the adults are to marry and that he will have to obey 16 (40%). At the circus Kingshaw is sick 16 (55%).

Why it matters. The marriage closes the household around him; even Hooper minds, but cannot stop it.

E34 Fielding at Warings 16 (60–95%)

Mrs Kingshaw arranges for Fielding to visit 16 (60%). At Warings Hooper draws Fielding away towards the Red Room and the attics, and Kingshaw sends Fielding home 16 (90%).

Why it matters. Kingshaw gives up his one friend rather than watch Hooper take him.

E35 The battle chart burned 16 (95–100%)

Kingshaw takes Hooper’s battle chart and lists of regiments and burns them by the copse 16 (95%); he expects to feel exultant and does not 16 (100%).

Why it matters. His one act of revenge gives him nothing.

Chapter 17

E36 The last note 17 (0–50%)

The night before school, a note is pushed under his door 17 (45%).

Why it matters. The second note answers the first: the novel’s arc runs between them.

E37 Hang Wood: the drowning 17 (55–90%)

In the morning Kingshaw walks to Hang Wood, takes off his clothes by the stream 17 (85%) and lies face down in the deepest water 17 (90%).

Why it matters. He chooses the place where he was safe and competent.

E38 Hooper finds him 17 (90–100%)

Hooper leads the search to the wood 17 (90%). Seeing the body, he feels triumph 17 (95%), and Mrs Kingshaw comforts him 17 (95%).

Why it matters. The novel ends on the survivor and the adult who still cannot see.

Characters

Charles Kingshaw

Almost eleven 2 (5%); his father is dead and his mother has come to Warings as Mr Hooper’s housekeeper 2 (0%).

  • Reserved and used to defeat: he expects to lose 4 (70%).
  • Afraid of water since a boy called Turville dared him into a swimming pool 3 (35%), yet happy swimming in the stream 7 (45%).
  • Silent under attack: he does not scream at the crow or shout in the Red Room 3 (55%).
  • Competent and responsible in the wood 7 (80%) 7 (85%).
  • Morally firm on the castle wall 12 (95%), but disbelieved by every adult 13 (0%).

Arc. From a wary arrival to brief confidence in Hang Wood, then to hopelessness and death.

Edmund Hooper

Joseph Hooper’s son and heir to Warings; his mother is dead 2 (80%).

  • Hostile from the first day 2 (25%).
  • Watchful and patient: his attacks use what he has seen 6 (25%).
  • Frightened of storms, the dark and heights 8 (55%) 12 (75%).
  • A fluent liar to adults 10 (0%), and triumphant at the end 17 (95%).

Arc. His power falters only in the wood and on the wall; by the end it is greater than before.

Joseph Hooper

Edmund’s father, owner of Warings, a widower.

  • Concerned with family line and appearances 1 (5%).
  • Uses stock phrases instead of attention 4 (20%).
  • Absolves everyone after the fall 13 (75%), yet is the one adult who physically comforts Kingshaw 13 (100%).

Arc. His courtship of Mrs Kingshaw advances as the boys’ situation worsens.

Mrs Helena Kingshaw

Charles’s widowed mother, housekeeper at Warings.

  • Sees the move as her chance 2 (90%).
  • Speaks in endearments and stock phrases 16 (60%) 10 (85%).
  • Turns towards Mr Hooper and away from her son 15 (35%).

Arc. From anxious newcomer to fiancée; she ends comforting Hooper beside her son’s body.

Fielding

A farmer’s son Kingshaw meets while Hooper is in hospital.

  • Easy, unafraid and practical 14 (60%).
  • Cannot understand the danger 14 (80%).
  • Sent away by Kingshaw when Hooper draws him in 16 (90%).

Arc. Hill’s Afterword calls him the one character who has known love, and says he has no power to save the others (Afterword).

Hill’s methods

AO3: how the writer creates meanings and effects through language, structure and form.

Third-person narration that moves between minds

The narrator enters Kingshaw, Hooper and the adults in turn, often within a chapter, so the reader knows what each hides from the others. Compare Hooper’s calculation 2 (10%) with the adults’ self-congratulation 4 (100%).

Juxtaposition of scenes

Hill cuts between a child’s distress and adult comfort without comment, as when Kingshaw’s hopes at night give way to the adults in the car 13 (70%).

Flat, plain sentences at moments of crisis

The most important actions get the shortest sentences 12 (95%) 12 (100%). The reader supplies the feeling the prose withholds.

Repetition and motif

The crow returns as a stuffed object 3 (55%); notes open and close the conflict 2 (25%) 17 (45%); water comforts and then kills 9 (85%) 17 (90%).

Stock phrases as characterisation

The adults speak in ready-made formulas 4 (20%) 16 (60%), which stand in for observation.

Foreshadowing

Kingshaw’s dream of falling into the sea 13 (80%) and his wish to stay in the stream 9 (85%) anticipate the ending.

Capitals and written notes

Hooper’s messages are written, printed and unexplained 2 (25%) 17 (45%).

Motifs and symbols

Moths and the Red Room

Dead things kept behind glass 1 (70%); the room becomes a prison 3 (70%), and Kingshaw remembers it near the end 16 (80%).

Crows

Alive in the cornfield, stuffed on the bed 3 (55%), and remembered when he goes near the attics 16 (85%). Kingshaw also imagines Mr Hooper as a crow 5 (75%).

The castle and the title

A playground rhyme made literal at Leydell Castle 12 (5%) 12 (35%); Kingshaw thinks of himself as King of the Castle when he imagines Warings without Hooper 13 (70%).

Hang Wood and water

The place of escape and competence, and finally of death 10 (100%) 17 (90%). LitCharts contrasts the stream with the swimming pool of Kingshaw’s past, where a boy called Turville made him dive 3 (35%): Kingshaw remembers the pool’s artificial blue when he hesitates at the stream 7 (45%), and then finds the wild water different.

School and uniform

The new school is the trap that closes 11 (35%); the uniform makes Kingshaw look like Hooper 15 (85%).

Past questions

Twenty past questions on the novel (Cambridge 0486, 2014–2016), arranged in chapter order. Each extract is mapped to the novel by chapter and position.

How to use these for the 2028 paper. Every paper here offered a passage-based question. From 2028 Paper 1 sets two essay questions on each prose text and no passage-based question (syllabus pp. 9, 36). Use the extract questions for close-reading practice, since an essay paragraph still needs this kind of analysis of a moment, and treat the essay alternatives as the closest match to the current paper.

Essay questions most likely to recur

Across these papers the essay alternatives return to the same subjects: Fielding (four times), Mrs Kingshaw (twice), Mr Hooper (twice), sympathy for Hooper, the adults, Warings, Leydell Castle, the countryside, dead creatures, blame for the death and its inevitability. The theme arcs and character profiles are built to cover each of these.
